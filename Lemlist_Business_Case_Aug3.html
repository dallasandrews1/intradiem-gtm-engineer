The case for Lemlist

We've built an outbound engine with a head start most teams would need a quarter to assemble: Clay decides who to contact and why, the Claude layer writes and quality-gates the copy, and our reps stay in control of every send. What's missing is the arm that executes the sequences across email, LinkedIn, phone, and WhatsApp. That's Lemlist. The campaigns are already built inside it on a 14-day trial. This is the case for keeping it.

Why this tool

It completes a system we've already invested in

We already pay for Clay, and it's doing its job: sourcing, enriching, and gating the right contacts at the right accounts. Clay is the front half of the motion. Lemlist is the back half, the layer that takes a finished, verified contact list and runs the actual conversation across channels. Without it, everything the front half produces gets sent by hand, one message at a time, and the motion caps at hand speed.

  • One tool runs the whole sequence. Email, LinkedIn visits and connects, call tasks, voice notes, and WhatsApp live in a single sequence that adapts to what the prospect does. If someone accepts a connect, the conversation moves to LinkedIn. If there's no phone number, the lead takes the email path automatically. No second tool, no manual handoffs between channels.
  • It's an AI tool, and it's built to be driven by one. Lemlist ships AI variables and sequence generation natively, and its API is how our Claude layer works: every campaign in the trial was built, loaded, and quality-checked programmatically. The reps run their campaigns themselves; the engine keeps them supplied.
  • Deliverability comes included. Mailbox warmup and custom tracking domains are bundled in the paid plan. Those are the pieces that keep cold email out of spam folders, and they're usually separate line items.
  • It fixes a defect we've already documented. Our previous sequencer kept emailing prospects after they had replied (it happened twice in wave 1) and double-enrolled ten contacts across campaigns. Lemlist stops the lead and everyone else at the same company the moment a reply lands. When we're emailing executives, that difference is reputation, not convenience.
  • The credits offset other spend. Three seats bring 4,500 enrichment credits a month, which takes real load off the Clay budget for verified emails and contact data.
Proof, not promise

The trial already did the work

This isn't a request to go evaluate a tool. The evaluation happened inside the free trial, and the output is staged and waiting:

US: three Star Ratings campaigns, staged

A re-engagement campaign and two fresh-pool campaigns (quality and finance lanes) with adaptive multichannel sequences, built for Nate as sender. Every loaded contact was hand-verified against the full customer list before load: zero current customers in the pool.

UK: two campaigns built for Jack

Insurance and financial services (62-contact first wave staged) plus airlines, on London business-hours schedules. Jack booked two meetings last week running outreach by hand; his seat is what that looks like at system speed, and he has his own build list starting with event follow-up sequences.

Visibility wired into Slack

When a prospect replies, the rep's channel gets the reply plus a drafted response waiting to be edited and sent. Meetings, bounces, and pending tasks surface the same day. Managers see the motion working in the open, not in a month-end report.

Guardrails that survive scale

Nothing sends without a person pressing start. Current customers are excluded and hand-verified before any load. Every claim in copy passes a verification gate. LinkedIn steps require human approval. Opt-out on every email. Daily send caps protect the rep's mailbox.

Why now

The trial ends August 13, and the campaigns are ready today

When the trial lapses it falls to a free tier that can't run multichannel sequences, and the staged campaigns sit idle. A yes converts to live sends within days: sign annual (asking the sender-slot question first), IT provisions the four mailboxes and the domain, Nate and Jack connect their accounts, and the reps press start on campaigns that already exist. The US Stars motion is also time-boxed by the measurement calendar; every week of delay is a week of the window we're writing to.

This is a head start, not a finished product. The build keeps going either way: more motions, more verticals, and the sequences Jack and Nate want to run themselves. What the licenses decide is whether the team operates the engine or keeps watching it in draft.
