# Dallas Andrews — Personal Brand Design Skill

This skill defines the emotional intent, design philosophy, and final-result vision behind every document Dallas creates. It works **in partnership with** the official `league-brand-assistant` skill (which provides color hex codes, font specs, and compliance rules). This skill provides the layer the official skill doesn't — the *why* behind the design, the feelings a document should evoke, and the standard the final result must meet.

> **How these two skills work together:**
> `league-brand-assistant` = the **rules** (what colors, what fonts, what not to do)
> `dallas-brand` = the **soul** (what it should feel like, what the viewer should experience, what "done" looks like)

Always load both. Apply the official brand rules first, then use this skill to elevate from "compliant" to "compelling."

## Part 1: Emotional Intent — How Documents Should Feel

Every document Dallas creates must hold a deliberate emotional duality. This is the core of his personal brand:

### The "Whimsical-But-Serious" Principle

Dallas's documents must simultaneously convey two energies that most people treat as opposites:

**Whimsical Energy:**
The viewer should feel creative intelligence at work. The design should feel *inventive* — like someone who sees possibilities others miss. There should be moments of unexpected delight: a subtle accent color, an elegant contrast shift, a layout that feels fresh rather than templated. This energy says: *"The person who made this thinks differently."*

**Serious Energy:**
The viewer should also feel precision and authority. The design should feel *engineered* — like every choice was deliberate. Clean lines, purposeful whitespace, clear hierarchy. Nothing decorative for its own sake. This energy says: *"The person who made this executes with discipline."*

**The duality in practice:**
These are not compromises. The document doesn't alternate between playful and serious — it holds both at once. The way a deep blurple cover transitions into warm creme interiors is the visual proof of this concept. Bold AND approachable. Confident AND warm. Innovative AND credible.

> *"The whole thing feels like it was designed by someone who works at an AI company, not generated."*

### Emotional Vocabulary

When evaluating whether a document meets this standard, apply these descriptors as a checklist. The document should feel:

| Descriptor | What It Means in Practice |
|---|---|
| **Whimsical** | Creative energy is visible in the design choices — unexpected color pairings, elegant accents, layouts that feel handcrafted rather than default |
| **Light** | Both literally (light backgrounds, ample whitespace) and emotionally (approachable, not heavy or corporate-stiff) |
| **Warm** | Color palette and spacing create an inviting tone — the viewer feels welcomed into the content, not confronted by it |
| **Airy** | Generous breathing room between elements — content is never cramped, never competing for space |
| **Clean** | Organized and purposeful — nothing extraneous, every element earns its place |
| **Impactful** | Design makes a strong first impression — the viewer remembers it, doesn't scroll past |
| **Rememberable** | Distinctive aesthetic choices (color palette, contrast pattern) stay with the viewer after they close the document |
| **Confident** | Bold design choices — deep colors, clear hierarchy, no hedging or tentative layouts |
| **Modern** | Contemporary and forward-thinking — feels like 2025+ design, not 2018 corporate |
| **Scannable** | Information architecture lets the viewer extract key points quickly without reading every word |
| **Purposeful** | Every design choice can answer "why is this here?" — nothing arbitrary |
| **Professional** | Executive-ready polish — zero question about credibility or competence |

If the document doesn't hit at least 10 of these 12 descriptors, it's not ready.

## Part 2: Design Philosophy — The Principles Behind the Choices

### 1. The Dark-to-Light Narrative Arc

Every multi-page document should follow this emotional structure:

**Opening (Cover / Title):** Dark, bold, commanding. Deep blurple backgrounds. The viewer's first impression is *confidence and authority.* This is the handshake — firm, direct, assured. The cover should "hit hard."

**Interior (Body Pages):** Light, warm, breathable. Warm creme backgrounds with soft accent colors. The viewer settles into the content feeling *welcomed and guided.* This is the conversation — warm, structured, generous with space.

**Closing:** Returns to a sense of completeness and intentionality. The viewer leaves feeling *"this was built by someone who thinks at a systems level."*

This dark→light→resolved arc mirrors how the best work operates: bold strategic vision, warm tactical execution, systems-level synthesis.

> *"Dark cover that hits hard, then light warm interiors that breathe."*

### 2. Intentional Contrast as Storytelling

Contrast is never accidental. Every shift in color, weight, or spacing tells a story:

- **Before/Now comparisons:** Muted gray for "before" states, bold blurple for "now" states. The transformation tells itself visually without the viewer needing to read every word.
- **Headers vs. body:** Deep blurple headers on periwinkle or creme backgrounds create clear hierarchy — the eye knows immediately what's a category and what's content.
- **Cover vs. interior:** The dramatic shift from dark to light signals "we're moving from overview to substance." It gives the document chapters without needing chapter markers.

> *"The transformation story tells itself visually."*

### 3. Whitespace as a Design Tool (Not Empty Space)

Whitespace is load-bearing. It does three things:

- **Creates breathing room:** Content surrounded by whitespace feels considered and premium. Content crammed into every corner feels desperate.
- **Guides the eye:** Whitespace creates natural reading paths. The viewer's attention flows where you intend.
- **Signals confidence:** Leaving space says "I don't need to fill every inch to prove my value." It's the visual equivalent of a leader who doesn't need to talk the most in a room.

Dead space (unused area with no purpose) is different from whitespace (intentional negative space). Dead space is a problem. Whitespace is a tool.

### 4. Visual Rhythm Without Clutter

Tables, rows, and content blocks should create a sense of rhythm — alternating backgrounds, consistent spacing, repeating accent patterns. But never busyness:

- **Alternating row colors** (e.g., periwinkle / creme) create scannable visual lanes
- **Consistent left-border accents** on key tables add structure without adding noise
- **Phase numbers, bullet accents, and header weights** create a cadence the eye follows naturally

The goal: the viewer should be able to *skim* the document and still absorb the structure and key points. If they have to read every word to understand the document, the design isn't doing its job.

### 5. Subtle Personality Touches

Small details differentiate "professionally designed" from "designed with soul." These include:

- A thin seafoam accent stripe on the cover — *"subtle but adds personality"*
- Blurple-mid left-border accents on risk tables
- Slightly deeper blurple tints alternating in metrics bars
- Lilac pill backgrounds for whimsical card elements

These touches should feel *discovered,* not announced. The viewer notices them on second look and thinks "oh, that's nice." They should never shout.

## Part 3: Final Result Vision — What "Done" Looks Like

### The SaaS Product Page Standard

The ultimate benchmark: **it should look like something you'd find on a premium SaaS company's website.** Not a corporate slide deck. Not a college paper. Not a generic template output.

Think: Stripe's documentation. Linear's product pages. Notion's landing page. The aesthetic quality that makes you trust the company before you've read a word — that's the target.

> *"Reads like something out of an actual SaaS product page."*

### The "AI Company Designed" Test

Before any document ships, apply this test:

**Does this look like it was designed by someone who works at an AI company, or does it look like it was generated by AI?**

"Designed by someone who works at an AI company" means:
- Intentional choices visible throughout
- Cohesive palette with purpose
- Systems-level thinking in the layout
- Personality and craft in the details
- Content and design reinforce each other

"Generated" means:
- Default layouts, default colors
- No visual narrative or progression
- Generic structure that could be about anything
- Technical accuracy but no soul
- Looks like the first thing an AI would spit out

The document must pass the first test, not the second.

### The User Experience Perspective

Think of the document as a product experience, not just a file:

**First 3 seconds (the cover):** The viewer decides if this is worth their time. The cover must create *intrigue and respect* simultaneously. They should think: "This person is serious AND creative."

**Next 30 seconds (the skim):** The viewer scans the interior pages. Visual rhythm, clear hierarchy, and intentional contrast should let them absorb the structure and key points without reading. They should think: "This is well-organized and well-designed."

**Full read (2-5 minutes):** The content and design reinforce each other. Every color choice, spacing decision, and layout pattern supports the narrative. They should think: "This person thinks at a systems level."

**After closing:** The document is *rememberable.* The blurple/creme palette, the whimsical-but-serious energy, the level of polish — it stays with them. They should think: "I want to show this to someone else."

### Audience Calibration

These documents are built for people who notice quality:

- **Silicon Valley executives** who see hundreds of decks and instantly filter for craft
- **C-suite leaders** who judge competence partly by presentation quality
- **Cross-functional partners** (like Marketing leaders) who have trained design eyes
- **Internal leadership** evaluating strategic thinking capacity

The design must signal: *"This person operates at a level above where they currently sit."*

This goes beyond hex codes (which live in `league-brand-assistant`) to explain what each color *communicates:*

| Color Family | Emotional Role | When to Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Deep Blurple** (#250D53) | Authority, innovation, confidence | Cover pages, section headers, bold data labels — anywhere you want the viewer to feel the weight of what's being said |
| **Blurple Text** (#19063A) | Clarity, seriousness, groundedness | Primary text on light backgrounds — the workhorse that says "this is considered, professional content" |
| **Blurple Mid** (#501CD2) | Energy, action, accent | Phase numbers, bullet accents, CTAs — the spark of whimsy within the serious framework |
| **Warm Creme** (#F9F7F6) | Warmth, approachability, breathing room | Interior page backgrounds — the emotional counterbalance to bold blurple covers |
| **Lilac** (#ECEAF1 / #D9D6E5 / #B4ADCA) | Structure, softness, organization | Table backgrounds, card elements, eyebrow text — adds order without adding weight |
| **Seafoam** (#DBEAEB / #B8D6D8 / #4E6B6D) | Personality, modernity, subtle delight | Accent stripes, divider elements — the "discovered detail" that adds character |
| **Periwinkle** (#F3F5FF / #E1E7FE) | Lightness, visual rhythm, scannability | Alternating row backgrounds, light card fills — creates lanes for the eye to follow |
| **Gray** (#666666) | Neutrality, supporting role, readability | Body copy on light backgrounds — recedes so bold elements can lead |

| Layout Pattern | Emotional Effect |
|---|---|

| Metrics bar with alternating dark tints | "Data with personality" — whimsical treatment of serious numbers |

## Part 5: What to Avoid

These are specific patterns that break the brand:

| Anti-Pattern | Why It Fails | What to Do Instead |
|---|---|---|
| All-dark design (monochromatic dark theme) | "Not enough contrast — we went from one extreme to the other." Feels heavy, oppressive, monotonous | Dark cover + light interiors. Use darkness for impact moments, not as the default |
| All-light design (no bold moments) | Feels generic and forgettable. No impression, no confidence | Ensure at least the cover and key section headers use deep blurple |
| Dead space (large unused areas) | Signals incomplete work or poor planning. Different from intentional whitespace | If a page is less than 70% utilized, tighten content or redistribute |
| Generic corporate template feel | Fails the "AI company designed" test. Looks like anyone could have made it | Apply the personal palette, accent details, and contrast narrative |
| Characters that don't render (fancy symbols) | Breaks the premium illusion. Shows the tool, not the design | Test every special character in the target font. Use standard bullets when in doubt |
| Font registration failures (bold not rendering) | Body text without proper weight differentiation looks flat and unprofessional | Always register font families with explicit normal/bold/italic/boldItalic mappings |
| Decorative elements with no purpose | Violates the "everything earns its place" principle | Every visual element must answer "what does this communicate?" |
| Cramped layouts | Signals desperation to fill space. Undermines the confidence the brand projects | Generous margins, padding, and breathing room between every element |

## Part 6: Quick-Start Checklist

Before sharing any document, verify:

- [ ] **Dark-to-light narrative:** Cover is bold/commanding, interiors are warm/breathable
- [ ] **Whimsical-but-serious duality:** Creative energy AND professional precision are both visible
- [ ] **Emotional descriptors pass:** Hits at least 10 of 12 (whimsical, light, warm, airy, clean, impactful, rememberable, confident, modern, scannable, purposeful, professional)
- [ ] **No dead space:** Every page is intentionally composed
- [ ] **Contrast tells a story:** Color shifts reinforce the content narrative
- [ ] **Subtle personality touches present:** At least 2-3 small details that reward a second look
- [ ] **Visual rhythm in tables/lists:** Alternating backgrounds, consistent accents, scannable structure
- [ ] **SaaS product page standard met:** Looks like it belongs on a premium tech company's website
- [ ] **"AI company designed" test passed:** Feels handcrafted and intentional, not generated
- [ ] **User experience arc works:** Cover creates intrigue → skim reveals structure → full read rewards → closing is rememberable
- [ ] **Audience-appropriate:** Would hold up in front of a Silicon Valley exec or C-suite leader
- [ ] **Fonts correct:** Outfit or Lato with proper family registration — no system font fallbacks

## How to Use This Skill

**For new documents:** Read this skill first to internalize the emotional targets. Then load `league-brand-assistant` for the technical specs. Design with soul first, compliance second — but ship with both.

**For reviewing existing work:** Run the Quick-Start Checklist. If anything fails, this skill tells you *what feeling is missing* — the brand assistant tells you *what rule to apply to fix it.*

**For explaining the approach to others:** The emotional vocabulary in Part 1 and the design philosophy in Part 2 give language to what this system does instinctively. When someone asks "why does this look so good?" — these are the answers.

**For collaborating with others:** Share this skill alongside `league-brand-assistant`. The brand assistant gives them the rules. This skill gives them the vision. Together, they can produce documents that don't just follow the guidelines — they *embody* the brand.
