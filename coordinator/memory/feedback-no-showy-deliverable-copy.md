On 2026-08-21, reviewing the Carter webinar brief (hero read "1,016 webinar registrants. Enriched, classified, routed in one afternoon."; TL;DR opened with "The machine is live"; credits quoted "about $12"), Dallas said: "stop making these titles and artifacts messaging be so arrogant and 'look at how impressive this is' style... this should be professional and direct not unnecessarily educational. people don't present things like that, that's not natural."

**Why:** the pages represent him to colleagues and leadership. Self-congratulation reads as insecurity and invites pushback; explainer tone ("persona is the product question...") talks down to the reader. A handover note from a peer states what was done, what the data shows, what is not done, and what is needed from the reader.

**How to apply:** headlines name the subject, not the feat ("Webinar registrants: enrichment, classification, routing", not "...in one afternoon"). No "the machine is live," "with no one in the loop," "powerful," "overnight," cost-as-a-brag, or "what you can do without asking anyone." No teaching asides. Short declarative sentences, numbers first, caveats stated plainly, next steps as open items pending the reader's decision. Same rule for artifact `description` text, Slack posts, and agendas. Pairs with [[feedback-no-failure-talk-leadership]] and [[positioning-automation-first-aug17]]: confident, factual, understated.

**Addendum, same day:** "every single line, sentence etc needs to add value in everything we create, otherwise it shouldn't be on the page." Before delivering any page, do a line-by-line pass and delete anything that only decorates, restates, or softens.

**Addendum 2 (Aug 21):** before sending a deliverable to a colleague, run the "would I find this useful if I were them, and their boss" check: (1) open with a cover note that states what was done, why it was done without asking (if so), and the one ask; (2) use the reader's vocabulary, never internal rubric keys; (3) when the work touched data another team owns, say it's reversible and how; (4) decisions the reader may not own become questions, including "who else should see this"; (5) cut a third. Dallas asked for exactly this on the Carter brief.

**Addendum 3 (Aug 21, Slack/DM drafts):** Dallas rejected a Naveen DM draft containing "Nothing sent yet" and "One place you could help" as "AI unnatural talk." DMs to Naveen or peers are two or three plain sentences: what he just did, for whom, the link, and "let's talk through next steps next week." No status disclaimers, no engineered asks, no summary bullets in chat messages.

**Addendum 4 (Aug 21, clarity test):** "If the artifact isn't simple and easy to understand what I did and what is created, then we've failed. Looking at this shouldn't create confusion or questions, it should invite excitement." The first screen must answer "what did Dallas make" at a glance: a few cards with one number each, a short visual of how it was built with counts at each step, then detail. No multi-column funnel tables, no "Tier 1/Tier 2" or "pool" jargon, decisions folded into one plain sentence. Professional and plain still applies; clarity is what makes it exciting.

**Addendum 5 (Aug 24, one-page rule):** reviewing the Naveen operating-process page (seven long phase cards, a messaging matrix, a three-week timeline, a replicable-cards section), Dallas: "is there not a less wordy, one-page version... we have to be impactful with these outputs and stop lessening our value by overexplaining all the time." Rule: a leadership deliverable is ONE page by default: the mechanism as one line per step (owner, output), the one artifact the reader acts on (a sheet, a table), and the decisions the reader owns. Everything that explains why, describes inputs, or narrates the timeline lives in the internal md, not on the page. Over-explaining reads as low confidence and lowers the perceived value of the work. Ask "would I send this as is?" before offering a link.
