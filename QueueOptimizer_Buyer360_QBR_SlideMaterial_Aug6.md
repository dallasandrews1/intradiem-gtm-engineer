## Why the QO slide is not just the BOO slide with new faces

If both slides look the same, the deck loses its best moment. The two buyers are in different places relative to us.

| | Back Office Optimizer | Queue Optimizer |
|---|---|---|
| Where the buyer sits | Mostly a room we have never been in. Back-office ops has no Intradiem badge on the desk | A room we are already standing in. Contact center ops, WFM, and the ACD owner are seats we work with today |
| The job of the slide | Teach reps who this person even is | Show reps the person they already know has a second reason to buy |
| Motion | New logos in the manual back-office white space | Install base first. Beta is first come, first served for existing customers |
| Seats in the buying committee | Four | Five. QO adds the SLA and penalty owner, and that seat is the forcing function |
| The rep's next move | Find the buyer | Ask the buyer we already have who owns the penalty exposure |

## The spine: five seats, constant across every vertical

| Seat | Role in the deal | What changes by vertical |
|---|---|---|
| **1. COO / VP Customer Care or Service Operations** | Economic buyer. Owns service levels, penalty exposure, and cost-to-serve. Can greenlight alone | Whether the title reads customer care, member services, service operations, or customer operations |
| **2. WFM / RTA leader** | Technical evaluator and the real champion. QO sits above their WFM and their ACD, and manual queue swaps are their day | How senior the WFM seat is. At the largest accounts it is a Sr Director with a bench; in retail it is a single manager |
| **3. Contact center ops director / service delivery** | Day-to-day champion. Lives the breach escalations and the overstaffing buffer | The function name: member services, claims contact center, care ops, online contact center |
| **4. Contact center technology / ACD platform owner** | Gatekeeper. Owns the ACD integration and approves anything that touches the routing layer | Which ACD they protect. This is where the Avaya line matters |
| **5. SLA / penalty owner** | The QO-only seat. Owns the contractual or regulatory consequence of a miss, so they carry the urgency the other four can defer | Healthcare: Stars and quality. Telecom: regulatory service obligations. Insurance and BPO: client contracts and vendor management. Retail and FS: no penalty, so this seat is absent and the hook shifts to buffer and overtime |

Tags: [IB] = named on the QO ICP account-evidence table, so we are already inside. [NN] = net new.

## The ACD line: Avaya, not Cisco

The QO ICP tech-stack line currently reads:

> Any ACD via integration: Genesys, Cisco, Five9, Amazon Connect

It should read:

> Any ACD via integration: Genesys, Avaya, Five9, Amazon Connect

The ICP card is the only place it's crossed. Every other page of the enablement material already lists the integration set correctly.

Suggested treatment on the slide: one row for **runs above Genesys, Avaya, Five9, Amazon Connect**, a separate row for the single-platform routing engines, so the two sets stay visually apart in anything re-cut downstream.

## Slide concepts

1. **Five seats, one row.** Same fixed-spine device as the back-office slide so the two read as one system, with the fifth seat visually distinct because it only exists where a penalty exists.
2. **The penalty light.** A small indicator per vertical: on for healthcare, insurance, telecom, and BPO, off for financial services and retail. When it is off, the hook line under the card changes from breach avoidance to buffer and overtime. One glance teaches reps not to run the wrong pitch.
3. **"Who does this by hand today."** Put Lance Owen's intraday-manager title on the slide as the artifact. A named human being whose job is manual queue moves is a stronger argument than any capability bullet.
4. **The badge already on the desk.** Every QO name here is a seat next to an account we already serve. Install-base badge on the QO card, shown against the BOO slide's mostly cold field.
5. **Avaya on the face of the card.** The integration row and the competitor row separated visually, so the Cisco crossover cannot happen again downstream in a deck someone re-cuts.
