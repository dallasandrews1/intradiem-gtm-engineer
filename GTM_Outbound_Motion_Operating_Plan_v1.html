The short version

The system, in five lines

  • The model: instead of waiting on tool approvals, I'm quarterbacking daily blitz sequences by hand. Each rep gets a morning brief in a dedicated Slack channel: exactly who to touch today, on which channel, with what copy. About an hour of their day, fully prepped.
  • The unit of work is a 10-day strike sequence: a full buying committee at one account hit across email, LinkedIn, and phone in a stacked cadence that's very hard to ignore politely. Two accounts in flight per rep at a time. Every sequence ends in a reply, a meeting, or a clean answer.
  • Four lanes are live or staged: Nate's US front office, Nate's net-new back office, Jack's Europe motion, and the install-base back office lane that hands 313 validated contacts to the account managers.
  • Visibility is built in, not reported after: the Slack channels are open. Anyone in leadership can scroll a channel and see that day's plays, sends, and replies without asking anyone for a status.
  • The manual phase is also the business case: every meeting booked by hand proves exactly what a sequencing tool automates. When the tooling lands, the playbook is already written and tested.
The operating model

Manual quarterback now, automated engine next

The strategy layer already runs on the agent system I've built: account research, strike sequence construction, copy, call scripts, and reply handling. What's manual is the last mile, and that's deliberate.

Every morning, Nate and Jack each get that day's plays dropped into their channel: the contacts, the channel for each touch, and the exact copy or talking points. They execute and flag replies; I reroute the sequence the same day a reply lands. The rep spends their hour selling instead of deciding what to do, and nothing depends on anyone remembering a cadence.

Clay stays what it's best at: the enrichment and context engine that feeds every sequence with verified contacts and account intelligence. It was never built to run multi-channel outreach, and we don't ask it to.

Targeting philosophy

Strategic outreach, not account-based blasting

Not every account gets a sequence. An account earns one when there's a problem big enough to be unignorable and evidence the timing is right.

For each candidate account we look for the forcing function: something public and current (an initiative, a stumble, a leadership change, a mandate) that connects to a problem we solve at a high level. When that connection exists, the whole sequence is built around shedding light on that problem, in the language of each seat on the buying committee. When it doesn't exist, we don't touch the account. The heat map decides where the next sequence goes, not the alphabet.

That's also why the cadence is stacked: email, LinkedIn, and phone landing together over ten days. A relevant message plus that kind of presence forces the question either way. Tell us to buzz off or take the meeting; both beat being ignored, and both are data.

The safety rule under every lane

A current customer never gets a cold touch. Every sequence account clears a customer-exclusion check against Salesforce plus an entity check on legal names and subsidiaries before anything sends, and the final check runs on real rows the morning of first send.

Star Ratings

The straight answer, and iteration two

Roughly 250 emails delivered against the Stars angle so far. The right conclusion isn't that the angle failed; it's that email-only can't prove or disprove an angle.

Delivered doesn't mean seen. A message that lands before 7 am is on page four of the inbox by the time the exec sits down, and nothing else in an email-only motion pulls them back to it. That's the ceiling of a single channel, not a verdict on Star Ratings.

Iteration two closes the loop instead of restarting it. Everyone already emailed moves into the multi-channel follow-through: calls that point back at the email, LinkedIn touches, and reply-to-thread sends with subject lines like "just called" that make the original message get read. The groundwork is already in Nate's hands: the Wave 1 send kit (42 contacts, all five touches, numbers defended inline) plus the first batch of about 50 cell numbers pulled through a ZoomInfo contact. The remaining numbers are part of the 6sense dig below.

Lesson carried forward

Sequencing matters as much as messaging: pilot the engine on angles that already work, then point it at experimental ones. Stars was the reverse order, which makes it a bad benchmark for the system and a fine experiment to finish properly. The front office lanes now run on proven ground first.

Tooling

The tool that automates what we're proving by hand

The manual system works and is producing now. What it can't do by hand is scale past a handful of accounts per rep, and that's exactly the job a sequencing tool does.

Lemlist is the fit: one campaign engine that runs the emails, the call steps, the LinkedIn touches, and the A/B tracking of what's working, the same multi-channel cadence we're proving manually. And it's absolutely an AI tool, which matters for how it gets positioned. Clay stays underneath it; Clay wasn't built to be an outreach engine, it's an enrichment engine, and the two don't overlap.

The path we're on: confirm trial capacity on Nate's side, run a small live campaign on the free tier, and let those results carry the case for paid seats. Proof first, ask second.
